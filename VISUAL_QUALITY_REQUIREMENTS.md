# 9/10 Visual Quality Requirements

## Purpose

This document is the fixed acceptance standard for the production quality of videos generated by this repository.

The target is **9/10 perceived visual quality** for a premium animated YouTube explainer/cartoon channel.

Passing tests, valid schemas, successful renders, cinematic metadata, or a technically correct pipeline do **not** prove 9/10 quality. The final rendered video is the evidence.

The channel ambition is that each upload feels like an **event**, not another automated-content notification. The system should be capable of supporting videos that viewers perceive as deliberately directed, visually distinctive, highly polished, and worth waiting for. No implementation can guarantee millions of views, but the production system must not impose an obvious visual or automation ceiling.

---

## 1. Definition of a 9/10 Video

A 9/10 result should create the impression:

> "This is a real premium animated YouTube brand."

It must **not** primarily create the impression:

- "This is impressive automation."
- "This is a generic Remotion template."
- "This is two puppets standing in front of interchangeable vector backgrounds."
- "This is a faceless-content pipeline with captions added on top."
- "The metadata sounds cinematic, but the pixels are not."

A 9/10 video does not require feature-film animation. It does require strong visual authorship, coherent art direction, readable acting, purposeful shot design, attractive composition, polished physical interactions, and enough variation that the viewer feels the episode was specifically directed rather than assembled from defaults.

---

## 2. Hard Acceptance Gates

A video cannot receive a 9/10 rating if **any** of the following are true:

1. A narratively important physical prop visibly floats, detaches from the hand, clips through the actor, or behaves like an icon card instead of an object.
2. Characters repeatedly appear in substantially identical frontal two-shots despite different dramatic beats.
3. A planned `reaction-closeup`, `prop-insert`, `over-shoulder`, `establishing`, `crossing-transition`, or `payoff-hold` does not create clearly different rendered pixels.
4. A character action intended for one actor visibly moves the whole cast.
5. A room/location is visually flat enough to read as a wall rectangle + floor rectangle + furniture icons.
6. Important environment dressing is hidden behind opaque background layers.
7. Character feet or props visibly float above, sink below, or inconsistently contact the environment.
8. Captions dominate the frame or make the production feel like a Shorts/TikTok template rather than long-form animation.
9. There is an obvious visual reset, teleport, or continuity break that is not an intentional edit.
10. Lip-sync is materially broken on normal viewing.
11. Audio has obvious joins, clipping, unstable loudness, or poorly motivated SFX.
12. The final payoff visually resembles an ordinary dialogue shot instead of a designed ending beat.
13. The first 5–10 seconds look generic, static, confusing, or visually cheaper than the rest of the episode.
14. The video contains repeated obviously templated compositions that make the automation mechanism easy to infer.
15. The full episode has not been rendered and visually inspected.

Any hard-gate failure means the video is **below 9/10 regardless of aggregate score**.

---

## 3. Scoring Rubric — 100 Points

A release candidate must score **90/100 or higher**, pass every hard gate above, and have no major category below the minimum stated below.

### 3.1 Hook and First-Impression Quality — 10 points

Minimum for 9/10 acceptance: **9/10**

Assess the first 5–10 seconds.

Requirements:

- Opening image is immediately understandable and visually intentional.
- At least one clear curiosity/tension object or action is visible quickly.
- The opening does not begin as a generic centered two-shot unless that framing is dramatically justified.
- Visual hierarchy makes the subject instantly readable on desktop and mobile.
- The first meaningful shot feels authored rather than auto-filled.
- Camera movement, if present, is motivated and controlled.
- The opening establishes a visual promise that the rest of the episode pays off.

Failure examples:

- Two static characters begin talking in the middle of a flat room.
- The hook depends entirely on narration/captions.
- Important visual information is too small to read.

---

### 3.2 Character Art and Channel Identity — 10 points

Minimum: **8.5/10**

Requirements:

- Main recurring characters have clearly different silhouettes.
- Face shapes, hair, clothing, proportions, palettes, and posture are recognizably distinct.
- Characters remain recognizable in wide, medium, and close framing.
- Line weights and shading feel deliberate rather than mechanically uniform.
- Clothing and facial construction contain enough dimensionality to avoid a flat clip-art appearance.
- Character design feels ownable as a recurring channel identity.
- Characters look coherent with the environment art style.
- No low-resolution, malformed, or visibly inconsistent SVG parts.

The test:

> If the channel name were removed, could a returning viewer plausibly recognize the recurring cast from a frame?

---

### 3.3 Character Acting and Performance — 12 points

Minimum: **9/12**

Requirements:

- Acting is actor-local; one character's action does not unintentionally transform the full cast.
- Dialogue meaning is readable from pose, expression, gaze, and timing—not only from captions.
- Pose vocabulary visibly distinguishes at least:
  - idle/listening,
  - explain/open-hand,
  - present/show object,
  - carry,
  - point,
  - surprise,
  - confusion/skepticism,
  - hesitation,
  - defeat,
  - reaction,
  - payoff/freeze.
- Blinks, gaze, breathing, head motion, and speaking motion add life without constant noise.
- Reactions occur at useful narrative beats instead of continuously.
- Listening characters perform rather than becoming statues.
- Walk/cross actions create clear spatial travel across the shot.
- Animation phase does not visibly restart at every dialogue clip unless an intentional cut hides it.

Failure examples:

- Ten gesture labels visually collapse to `arm-up` or `arm-down`.
- Both actors slide together during a walk-cross.
- Everyone faces camera despite supposedly interacting with one another.

---

### 3.4 Prop Physicality and Object Storytelling — 12 points

Minimum: **10/12**

Requirements:

- Important narrative objects behave like physical scene objects, not floating UI cards.
- Hand-held props use pose-specific palm anchors.
- Prop scale is consistent with the character and shot.
- Object z-order makes physical sense relative to hands, torso, foreground furniture, and camera.
- A carried object follows the correct actor through movement.
- `prop-insert` shots are genuinely object-dominant.
- Props support storytelling through visible state changes: grabbed, carried, placed, opened, connected, missed, left behind, etc.
- Rotation/perspective are plausible for the hand or surface.
- Important objects are readable at normal playback size.
- Badge/icon treatment is reserved for literal interfaces, notifications, scores, or abstract overlays.

The test:

> Could a silent viewer understand what the character is doing with the important object?

---

### 3.5 Shot Design and Composition — 14 points

Minimum: **12/14**

Requirements:

A long episode must visibly use multiple shot families where the story supports them:

- establishing,
- two-shot,
- speaker close-up,
- listener/reaction close-up,
- prop insert,
- over-shoulder,
- crossing/doorway transition,
- payoff/hero hold.

For 13+ content scenes:

- At least **4 clearly distinct framing families** must appear.
- At least **3 significantly different screen-space compositions** must appear even within repeated environments.
- Planned shot recipes must create visibly different pixels, not merely different metadata.
- Subject scale changes must be large enough to perceive immediately.
- Shot changes should support story beats rather than random variety.
- Rule-of-thirds, foreground framing, asymmetry, negative space, depth, or blocking should be used deliberately.
- Repeated conversations should alternate visual emphasis intelligently rather than default to fixed left/right slots every time.

A `reaction-closeup` that looks like a 5% zoomed two-shot fails this requirement.

---

### 3.6 Environment Art, Depth, and Lighting — 10 points

Minimum: **8.5/10**

Requirements:

- Environments have clear foreground, performance/midground, and background depth.
- Perspective is coherent: floor lines, room scale, doors, counters, furniture, windows, etc.
- Background layers do not paint over scene dressing that should be visible.
- Rooms contain restrained environmental storytelling instead of arbitrary decoration.
- Foreground masks/set-pieces integrate actors into the room.
- Contact shadows or grounding cues prevent characters from floating.
- Lighting direction and palette are coherent with the scene.
- Locations are visibly distinct, not recolored versions of the same stage.
- No oversized set piece unintentionally competes with the subject.
- Outdoor/interior scale is believable enough for the stylized art direction.

For 13+ scene episodes, generally require at least **3 motivated visible environments** unless the premise intentionally remains in one location.

---

### 3.7 Camera, Motion, and Transitions — 8 points

Minimum: **7/8**

Requirements:

- Camera movement has narrative purpose.
- Push-ins emphasize realization/reaction.
- Prop-focus movement serves an object insert.
- Doorway tracking follows crossing action.
- Payoff holds settle rather than continue unnecessary motion.
- Pans/tracking correspond to subject movement or reveal.
- No camera shake for the sake of activity.
- Easing feels controlled and deliberate.
- Transitions are subordinate to story—not decorative presets repeated mechanically.
- Hard cuts are allowed and encouraged where stronger than animated transitions.

---

### 3.8 Visual Continuity and Spatial Logic — 8 points

Minimum: **7/8**

Requirements:

- Character side/position remains understandable across connected shots.
- Carried objects remain with the correct character.
- Room changes are visually motivated.
- Doorway stories clearly show room A → threshold/crossing → room B.
- Set pieces appear only where spatially appropriate.
- Actor scale does not inexplicably change between related shots.
- Continuous actions do not reset or teleport unless hidden by an intentional editorial cut.
- Callback objects/locations visually connect setup and payoff.

---

### 3.9 Captions and On-Screen Typography — 6 points

Minimum: **5/6**

Requirements:

- Captions support comprehension without becoming the primary visual layer.
- Typography feels appropriate for long-form 16:9 content.
- Text is readable on mobile without oversized blocking boxes.
- Line wrapping is controlled.
- Captions avoid faces and important props.
- Outline/shadow treatment is clean and restrained.
- Emphasis is selective rather than constant karaoke styling.
- No text-card aesthetic unless the story explicitly calls for a card/interface.

---

### 3.10 Polish, Cohesion, and Automation Invisibility — 10 points

Minimum: **9/10**

Requirements:

- Color palette, outline treatment, shading, shadows, and prop style feel like one production.
- Visual density is intentional and does not oscillate between empty and cluttered scenes.
- No debug-looking overlays, placeholders, default icons, mismatched asset families, or obvious stock elements.
- Repeated assets are staged differently enough to avoid template recognition.
- Every major story beat appears visually authored.
- The episode has a recognizable signature style.

Automation detection test:

> Show the video without explaining how it was created. Would a reasonable viewer's first reaction be "this is automated/template-generated"?

If yes, this category cannot score above **6/10**, and the overall video cannot pass the 9/10 gate.

## 4. Adjacent Production Requirements

These are not purely visual, but obvious failures materially reduce perceived visual quality and therefore block a 9/10 release.

### Audio

- Dialogue is clean and consistent.
- Lip-sync remains aligned at normal playback speed.
- No audible AAC/scene-boundary gaps or clicks.
- SFX are motivated by visible actions, transitions, reactions, or payoff beats.
- SFX never bury dialogue.
- Final loudness target should remain approximately **-14 LUFS integrated**, with safe true peak (current production target: approximately `TP=-1.0`).
- Music, when used, should support pacing and identity rather than mask weak visuals.

### Editing/Pacing

- No long stretch feels visually dead.
- New visual information arrives often enough to sustain attention without becoming frantic.
- Cut frequency is not itself a quality metric; **shot contrast** is.
- Reactions, inserts, location changes, physical actions, and reframes should create visual progression.
- The final payoff must feel stronger/more resolved than the setup.

---

## 5. Event-Level Channel Standard

A technically polished video can still fail the channel goal if it is forgettable.

For an upload to feel like an event, it should satisfy most of the following:

- The opening contains a strong visual premise or contradiction.
- The central visual idea is easy to remember after viewing.
- The recurring cast/channel style is recognizable.
- At least several moments feel specifically designed for this episode rather than reusable generic beats.
- There is escalation: visuals become more revealing, surprising, absurd, emotionally clear, or satisfying as the story develops.
- Callback/payoff imagery rewards viewers who watched the setup.
- At least one frame or visual beat could plausibly become a memorable thumbnail/clip/reaction moment.
- The video contains enough visual authorship that rewatching reveals details or makes the joke/story land again.
- The final shot feels intentionally composed as an ending image.

A 9/10 implementation cannot guarantee virality, retention, CTR, or millions of views. It should, however, remove production quality as the obvious reason the channel cannot compete at that level.

## 6. Minimum Shot-Diversity Contract for New Episodes

For a normal 13+ scene cartoon episode, unless story constraints justify an exception:

- >= 4 distinct framing/shot families.
- >= 2 motivated camera-motion types.
- >= 3 motivated environment/location+variant combinations.
- >= 1 establishing beat.
- >= 1 reaction close-up.
- >= 1 physical prop insert when a central object exists.
- >= 1 transition/crossing/location-change beat when the story involves movement between places.
- >= 1 payoff hold or clearly distinct final composition.
- No more than 5 consecutive scenes with effectively identical staging.
- No long run where metadata changes but visible actor positions/scale/depth remain essentially unchanged.

The compiler/renderer must preserve validated planner diversity rather than canonicalizing it back to generic medium/two-shot defaults.

---

## 7. Character Performance Contract

Recurring character animation must preserve:

- layered rig architecture,
- Rhubarb-compatible mouth cues,
- eye/pupil animation,
- facial-expression states,
- stable scene/actor identity,
- motion offsets across dialogue clips where needed,
- actor-local transforms,
- pose-specific hand anchors.

Preferred pose vocabulary should continue expanding beyond binary arm states where the rendered video demonstrates a need.

Character upgrades must not trade away reliable lip-sync merely for prettier static illustrations.

---

## 8. Prop Contract

Physical narrative props should default to `physical`, not `badge`.

Required behavior where relevant:

- hand-held → follows pose-specific hand anchor,
- carried → follows the owning actor's local transform,
- on-table/on-counter → grounded to surface and correct z-order,
- floor → grounded to scene perspective,
- wall-mounted → stable against the environment,
- background set piece → belongs to the set rather than floating in actor space,
- UI badge → reserved for actual UI/abstract overlay semantics.

Prop inserts must be visually distinct enough that a still frame immediately reads as an insert.

---

## 9. Background and Layering Contract

The renderer must preserve a coherent stack such as:

1. base/back environment,
2. layered background elements,
3. visible scene dressing,
4. actors and physical props,
5. foreground masks/set pieces where appropriate,
6. lighting/cinematic accents,
7. captions/UI overlays.

Opaque background layers must not unintentionally hide scene dressing.

Foreground elements must not unintentionally cover faces or narratively important props.

---

## 10. Benchmark and Evidence Procedure

### Fixed benchmark

Use the persisted benchmark episode beginning with:

`run_385203f1`

when comparing renderer changes so improvements are attributable to implementation rather than a different story.

### Fast loop

Use representative still/contact-sheet rendering for:

- composition,
- character art,
- environment depth,
- prop placement,
- pose correctness,
- shot contrast,
- lighting,
- z-order.

The fast preview should deliberately sample multiple cinematic recipes rather than only one location or shot family.

### Short motion loop

Use short clips for:

- actor motion,
- camera movement,
- transition timing,
- gesture timing,
- prop-follow behavior,
- animation continuity.

### Full MP4 checkpoint

A full episode render is mandatory before merge/release to assess:

- overall pacing,
- shot rhythm,
- lip-sync,
- audio joins,
- SFX timing,
- caption behavior,
- transition continuity,
- hook and payoff,
- automation/template feel.

### Inspection evidence

At a full checkpoint, record at minimum:

- rendered commit SHA,
- benchmark run ID,
- MP4 duration/resolution/fps,
- contact sheet/key frames,
- category scores from this document,
- hard-gate failures if any,
- top 1–2 remaining visible defects,
- comparison to previous iteration.

Do not increase a quality score solely because code, schema, metadata, or tests changed. The score must follow visible/audible evidence.

---

## 11. Iteration Policy

Use empirical iteration:

`small targeted change → render representative output → inspect → identify largest remaining visible defect → repeat`

Rules:

- Change at most 1–2 tightly related visual problems per fast iteration.
- Do not repeatedly rewrite components that already score well.
- Prefer fixing the lowest-scoring/highest-impact category.
- Keep full MP4 renders for meaningful checkpoints rather than every micro-change.
- Do not merge a visual-quality PR merely because CI is green.
- Do not declare 9/10 without a visually inspected full MP4.

---

## 12. Release Decision

A video qualifies as a **9/10 release candidate** only when all are true:

- All hard acceptance gates pass.
- Weighted score is >= 90/100.
- Hook score >= 9/10.
- Character acting >= 9/12.
- Prop physicality >= 10/12.
- Shot design >= 12/14.
- Automation-invisibility/polish >= 9/10.
- No major visual category feels obviously unfinished.
- Full MP4 has been watched, not merely sampled as stills.
- The final video looks deliberately directed at normal playback speed.
- The result feels closer to a premium recurring animated channel than to an automated template.

If there is doubt between 8/10 and 9/10, treat the candidate as **8/10 and continue iterating**.
