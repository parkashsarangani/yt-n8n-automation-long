# Quiet Confidence: retention review and repeatable editorial workflow

The implementation before this review established a curriculum, not a reliable high-retention editorial process. It had conflicting appended prompts, retired visual-director instructions, label-only validation, and a release bug that allowed a critic's `revise` verdict to pass on numeric scores. No live episode or audience data establishes viral potential.

## Corrections

- Replaced the active packaging/story/script/critic prompts with coherent audio-first versions. Historical versions remain immutable.
- Kept episode objectives stable while allowing public titles to be stronger than curriculum labels. Three packaging families remain inspectable in the stored growth package. Hook alternatives are a private drafting instruction, not an implemented multi-generation experiment.
- Required early useful progress: the writer aims for an initial response/contrast by words 65–75; runtime rejects a first response starting after 90 words. This is a production heuristic, not a YouTube threshold or measured timing.
- Replaced forced low-point/vindication arcs with plausible dialogue, causal progression, harder applications and a concrete payoff. Revision directives now support both practical lessons and stories.
- Required spoken content, no spoken planning tags, a scenario opening and a separate final outro. This prevents the release worker from replacing the substantive payoff with the continuation line. Semantic quality still requires editorial judgment; tags and word counts cannot establish usefulness.
- Made critic verdicts binding: `revise`, `abandon`, missing verdict and explicit abandonment cannot pass through high scores. Updated evaluator policy identity so earlier adjudications cannot stand in for the changed policy.

## Repeat for every new episode

1. Select its fixed listener problem and learning objective.
2. Compare curiosity, conflict and reversal packaging. Record the choice and its main risk in selection_rationale. Keep the title/thumbnail/opening on one promise.
3. Work backwards from the payoff. Build distinct acts with early value, contrasting responses, a harder application, limitations and a feasible exercise. Do not force the same surface structure every time.
4. Draft spoken narration. Audition opening phrasing privately; give the listener value before requesting sustained attention. Fully explain hypothetical examples through audio.
5. Run mechanical checks and the independent critic. The critique must locate an exit risk and prescribe an edit. Revision is bounded and cannot silently promote a rejected script.
6. Review an actual generated pilot before expanding production. Listen without looking at the screen; check delivery, pauses, speaker clarity and whether the answer feels earned.
7. After publishing, inspect available CTR, early retention, average percentage viewed and retention dips/spikes alongside comparable episodes. Treat small samples and unavailable measurements honestly. Track subscriber conversion and next-episode use separately where analytics makes them available; this change does not add those integrations.

## Opening illustration (editorial example, not a tested winner)

Topic: interruptions. An illustrative hook is: “Imagine you're halfway through your point when a colleague talks over you. You stop. Now everyone is answering them. How do you get the floor back without starting a fight?”

Deliver an initial response quickly, then explore why the same wording may work differently with a peer, a manager or repeated interruptions. Do not promise instant respect. The payoff returns to the original moment and demonstrates a usable choice, including when to disengage.

## Evidence and limits

YouTube describes intro retention as the proportion still watching after thirty seconds and recommends examining whether the opening matches the title/thumbnail promise and moving compelling later material earlier:
https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en

YouTube describes recommendations using multiple signals including satisfaction, not watch time alone:
https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16089387?hl=en

These sources support the review direction, not a guaranteed distribution outcome. The 65–75/90-word bounds and 30–60-second drafting rhythm are our editable heuristics. This implementation does not independently fact-check claims, run an audience hook experiment, guarantee originality across arbitrary future seasons, or guarantee viral growth. The selected eight-episode curriculum is versioned; another season requires a new catalog and corresponding schema support.
