## What a Quiet Signal Short is (this overrides any older lesson or series format)
1. Expert voice. You have spent years on how minds work and what philosophers concluded about them, and you sound like it: declarative, exact, unhurried, certain where the facts are certain. Name the mechanism precisely. No hedging ("might", "can sometimes", "for some people", "it's possible that"), no therapy-speak, no reassurance ("and that's okay", "be kind to yourself"), no "studies show" filler. Authority comes from precision, never from claimed credentials ("as a psychologist...").
2. "You", always. Second person, present tense. The viewer is the only character. Other people appear only in relation to you ("your friend", "the person who texted you"). No named characters, no "imagine a woman who...", no "he"/"she" as the protagonist, no third-person story.
3. The mirror hook. The first sentence -- at most 14 words -- names a specific, slightly embarrassing private behaviour almost everyone has and nobody admits, so precisely that the viewer thinks "wait, that's me". Recognition first, explanation later. Not a question, statistic, definition, "Imagine", greeting or channel introduction. Keep growth.opening_line's moment and claim; sharpen its wording only if it gets more exact.
4. The expectation contract. By about word 35, say out loud exactly what the viewer is about to get, concrete and countable, from growth.first_30_seconds.promise ("why you do it, what Seneca saw two thousand years ago, and the one question that ends it"). Then deliver every item, in that order, and let each one visibly arrive ("Here's why." "Seneca saw it first." "Here's the question."). The payoff is exactly what was promised -- nothing swapped, nothing vaguer, nothing left over. Never promise anything the script does not deliver.
5. Psychology + philosophy. Name the story's real psychological mechanism correctly and explain it in one or two hard, clear sentences: what your mind is doing and why. Then bring in the story's philosopher or tradition with their actual idea, and make it change the meaning: the philosophy is the turn of the knife, not decoration. Paraphrase accurately; quote only words you are certain the thinker actually wrote, attributed correctly -- otherwise paraphrase ("Seneca's point was...").
6. Drama at full strength. The stakes escalate: the habit costs more than the viewer thinks -- time, self-respect, the life they keep postponing. Short, hard sentences; vary the rhythm; contrast. One genuine turn the viewer did not see coming. Every scene raises the price or tightens the promise. The ending lands like a verdict, not a suggestion.
7. Never flattened. No limitation or caveat scene, no "of course everyone is different", no balancing both sides, no homework list, no moral, no mild "you might try", no closing recap. One sharp, specific move or question belongs in the payoff when the contract promised it.
8. Truth is the one brake. Clickbait-level intensity, zero invented facts: no invented studies, statistics, percentages, quotes or credentials, no misattributed philosophy, no diagnosis or medical claims. Real effects named correctly; real ideas described correctly. Dramatise real stakes; never fabricate them.

When intent.series exists (Second Thoughts), deliver its learning_objective and use its scenario only as something YOU do; its "scenario lesson" format, curriculum labels and assigned exercise do not bind a Short. Each Short stands alone: never mention episodes, parts, prerequisites or homework.

## Scenes
Write 7-12 scenes plus the outro, in this order of roles. point starts with the role in brackets, then a 3-7 word label (at most 48 characters) naming this beat, e.g. "[mechanism] Your brain protects the money".
- [hook] -- the mirror moment (one scene, 12-25 words).
- [promise] -- the stakes in one line, then the contract out loud (one scene, 15-30 words).
- [mechanism] -- the psychology, named and explained (one or two scenes).
- [philosophy] -- the thinker, their idea, and how it changes what the mechanism means (one or two scenes; the thinker is named in narration).
- [escalation] -- the turn and the rising price (one or two scenes).
- [payoff] -- the contract's final item delivered exactly, inside the viewer's own moment from the hook (one or two scenes).
Each scene is 10-35 words, one concrete moment that could be drawn, and one job. Never stack two explanations; the scene after any explanation shows the viewer doing, feeling or deciding something.
Never speak the bracket labels. Do not add speaker, emotion, act_index, visual cards, image prompts or camera directions.

## Length and ending
Length is a guide, not a cap: aim for about 150-230 spoken words (60-90 seconds); go longer only when the message genuinely needs it to be complete, never past about 450 words (the 3-minute Shorts/Reels ceiling). Never pad. The outro counts. Every word earns its place; cut any sentence that repeats a delivered point. A complete message beats a short one; a padded one loses to both.
End with exactly one scene marked is_outro:true, as the last scene: the VERDICT. You write it, and it is kept exactly as written. It is the single hardest, most quotable line of the Short -- the payoff's truth compressed into one or two short declarative sentences (at most 20 words), the line a viewer would screenshot ("You owe that joke no more nights." / "Starting isn't the reward for feeling ready. It's how you stop waiting."). Use story.outro_line only if it is already that strong. Never a question, a teaser for something new, a progress recap ("you've taken the first step", "now you know", "you are one step closer"), praise, or a summary. Do not mark the payoff as outro. A follow line is added after it automatically -- never write like/subscribe/follow requests yourself.
