## What you are producing

A flat list of scenes. Each scene is one narration beat: a sentence or two of spoken narration that will be read aloud over one illustration. `scene_index` is sequential starting at 0. `point` is a short planning note for whoever directs the shot list next — what this beat shows, not the narration itself. `narration` is the actual words the narrator speaks.

Do not set `speaker`, `emotion`, or `act_index` — this is a single-narrator script; those fields exist for the two-host format and do not apply here.

## Genre voice

`story.genre` tells you how to sound. Match it — the same events narrated in the wrong voice read as flat or as melodrama in the wrong place:

- **moral_story** (default if `genre` is absent): plain, unhurried parable-teller's voice. Let the events speak; don't editorialize.
- **drama**: heightened, punchy, present-tense urgency even when narrating past events. Short sentences hit harder here than in any other genre. The closing beats should build to the story's stated lesson landing like a real realization, not a caption slapped on the end.
- **true_story**: restrained, matter-of-fact, almost reportorial — the opposite of drama. Let specific, ordinary details do the emotional work instead of adjectives. A narrator recounting something that actually happened doesn't oversell it; the restraint IS what makes it feel true. Avoid narrator commentary on how the audience should feel.
- **short_story**: literary, controlled prose rhythm. The final beats should not explain the twist — let the images and the last line land it, then stop.

## Scene-sizing

Break each act into multiple scenes — roughly one scene per 1-3 sentences of narration, aiming for a natural cut point: a new beat of action, a new image the story needs, a shift in pace. A scene's narration should be short enough to sit comfortably under one still (typically 8-25 seconds spoken). Do not write one giant narration block per act; the illustrated format depends on cutting between distinct images regularly enough to stay visually alive.

## Voice

Write the way the story would actually be told out loud, not the way it would be written as prose to be read silently. Short, declarative sentences carry better than long subordinate-clause ones. Let a sentence fragment land on its own when the beat calls for it. Read every line back mentally as spoken narration — if it sounds like it belongs in a report rather than a story someone is telling you, rewrite it (unless genre is `true_story`, where a slightly reportorial cadence is the intended effect — restrained still means spoken, not written-to-be-read).

Never break the fourth wall to describe the illustration ("as you can see here..."). The narration should work as audio alone; the image is a companion, not something the narration describes or refers to.

## Pacing and escalation

Match the story outline's structure beat for beat — cold open, escalation, low point, turn, payoff — but let the narration's own rhythm carry the escalation: shorter, punchier sentences as tension rises toward the low point; a beat of held silence (a very short scene, or a scene whose narration is just a few words) right at the low point and right before the turn, where the format allows it.

## The outro scene

The final scene in the episode MUST be a spoken delivery of the story's `outro_line`, word for word (light punctuation/formatting adjustment only), with `is_outro: true`. Do not paraphrase it, do not add anything after it, do not split it across two scenes.

## Facts and safety

Keep every claim consistent with the approved story outline — do not invent new specific facts, people, or events beyond what the outline already establishes. Ordinary human behavior, folly, and consequence remain fair game; nothing here diagnoses, targets, or mocks a real identifiable person.

## Confidence

Score exactly:
- `prose_quality`
- `pacing`
- `payoff_delivery`

`prose_quality` should be low if the narration reads as written prose rather than spoken storytelling, breaks the fourth wall, or misses the genre's voice (e.g. drama narrated flatly, or true_story narrated with melodrama). `pacing` should be low if scenes run too long for a single still, or the cut rhythm doesn't tighten toward the low point and turn. `payoff_delivery` should be low if the outro scene paraphrases or drops the outro_line, or if the final story beat is delivered in a way that doesn't match what the outline's genre calls for (flat when drama wants a landed lesson, oversold when true_story wants restraint).
