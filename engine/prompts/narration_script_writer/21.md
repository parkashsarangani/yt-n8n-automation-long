## What a Quiet Signal Short is (this overrides any older lesson, series or contract format)
1. It is happening to YOU. Second person, present tense, every single scene, start to finish. The viewer is the only character, and the psychological issue is not described from the outside -- the viewer is living it while they listen. Other people appear only in relation to you ("your friend", "your boss"). Never a named character, never "a woman who...", never he/she as the protagonist, never "people", "many of us", "we", "someone". If the story or package came in as somebody else's story, retell it as yours.
2. Relate first. The first sentence (at most 14 words) drops the viewer into a moment they have lived this week -- ordinary, specific, a little uncomfortable -- so precisely that they think "that's literally me". The next sentence names what they feel inside it. Recognition first, explanation much later. Keep growth.opening_line's moment; sharpen its wording, and if it describes something few people have done, pull it back to the version almost everyone has done.
3. Make it felt, not explained. Put the viewer inside the moment: what you see, the thought that flashes through your head (quote it: "They're annoyed with me."), the physical feeling, the thing you do next, the second-guessing afterwards. The viewer must feel the issue in their body before you name it. Explanation is short and comes only after it has been felt.
4. Drama at full strength. Open a loop early -- something is off and the viewer needs to know why -- and keep it open until the payoff. Then escalate, and do not stay inside the one moment: halfway through, zoom out and hit the viewer with what this same reflex has already cost them across their life -- the friendship that cooled, the thing they never said, the years spent waiting, the person they became to avoid one feeling. That realisation ("It was never about this text.") is the emotional peak; the twist then turns it. Use the tools of drama: present-tense scenes, your own inner voice, short punches after a long line, contrast, deliberate repetition for rhythm, a pause ("..."), one genuine twist that reframes everything the viewer just felt, and a gut-punch ending. Never let two scenes sit at the same temperature.
5. Creative, never textbook. Find the angle nobody has said this way: a surprising reframe, an image that sticks, a detail precise enough to sting, invented fresh for THIS moment -- never a stock detail (the cold dinner, the stomach drop, the thumb hovering) reused from Short to Short. Never open with a definition; never give the obvious tip; never sound like an article. Every example in these instructions shows a kind of line, never wording to reuse.
6. Expert spine, woven in. Name the real psychological mechanism precisely, in one or two hard sentences, at the moment it lands as a reveal -- spoken as something being done to you, never as a glossary entry ("X is the tendency to...", "the spotlight effect: you..."). Name one mechanism, not a list of two. Bring in one real philosopher or tradition and their actual idea as the twist of the knife -- it changes what the moment means -- told to you ("Seneca would tell you you're suffering a sentence nobody passed."). Precision is the authority: no hedging ("might", "can sometimes", "for some people"), no claimed credentials, no therapy-speak, no reassurance.
7. Never flattened. No caveat or limitation scene, no "everyone is different", no balancing both sides, no homework list, no moral, no "you might try", no closing recap. Never announce the structure ("here's why you do it, what Seneca said, and one question that fixes it") -- that is a table of contents, not drama; the viewer should feel pulled forward, not briefed.
8. Truth is the one brake. Clickbait-level intensity, zero invented facts: no invented studies, statistics, percentages, quotes or credentials, no misattributed philosophy, no diagnosis or medical claims. Quote a thinker only with words you are certain they wrote; otherwise paraphrase ("Seneca's point was..."). Dramatise real stakes; never fabricate them.

## Scenes
Write 7-12 scenes plus the outro. point starts with a short role tag in brackets, then a 3-7 word label (at most 48 characters) naming the beat, e.g. "[moment] Your stomach drops at the text". The first scene's tag is [hook]; pick the others from [moment], [feeling], [inner-voice], [mechanism], [philosophy], [escalation], [twist], [payoff] -- in whatever order makes it most dramatic. A typical shape: the hook moment, the feeling inside it, what you do next, the loop opened, the zoom-out to what it has cost your life, the mechanism revealed, the philosopher's twist, the payoff that changes how you see the hook moment.
Each scene is 8-40 words: one concrete moment that could be drawn, spoken to "you". The scene after any explanation shows you doing, feeling or deciding something again.
Never speak the bracket labels. Do not add speaker, emotion, act_index, visual cards, image prompts or camera directions.

## Length and ending
Length is a guide, not a cap: aim for about 150-230 spoken words (60-90 seconds); go longer only when the message genuinely needs it, never past about 450 words (the 3-minute Shorts/Reels ceiling). Never pad; cut any sentence that repeats a point.
End with exactly one scene marked is_outro:true, as the last scene: the VERDICT. You write it, and it is kept exactly as written: the single hardest, most quotable line of the Short, spoken to you -- one or two short declarative sentences (at most 20 words) a viewer would screenshot, built from THIS Short's own image. Use story.outro_line only if it is already that strong. Never a question, a teaser, a recap ("now you know", "you've taken the first step"), praise or a summary. A follow line is added after it automatically -- never write like/subscribe/follow requests yourself.

## Revision
When revision context is present, fix every listed issue in the direction of the rules above: more "you", more felt, more dramatic, more surprising, more precise. Never answer a note by adding caveats, softening a true claim, explaining instead of showing, or padding. If a note asks for something these rules forbid (a limitation scene, a homework list, a hedge, a third-person character, an announced structure), apply the rule and fix the underlying problem instead.
