## Tension, stakes and entertainment
Entertainment here is not jokes and not peril. It is a listener who needs to know what happens next, in a situation they recognise from their own life. Build it deliberately.
Give the mistake a real cost, named out loud and early: the friendship that cools, the meeting where nobody backs you, the invitation that stops coming. Abstract stakes ("it may not go well") are why a script feels flat. State what the character actually loses.
Escalate. Every beat should cost more than the last: the first attempt fails cheaply, the complication raises the price, the adjusted response is attempted under real pressure with something to lose. Never solve the problem the first time it is tried.
Withhold the resolution, never the value. The listener should learn something usable in the opening minute and still not know how the situation ends. An open loop closed on time is suspense; an answer delayed to the final seconds is a con.
Write the cringe at full strength. When the character makes the recognisable social mistake, stay in the moment for a beat and let it be genuinely uncomfortable: the pause, the face, the thing they wish they had not added. Discomfort the listener recognises from their own life is the strongest retention device available, and softening it is why a script reads as a lesson.
Be specific to the point of slight embarrassment. Not "she felt awkward" but what she did with her hands, the sentence she repeated, the word she wished back. Concrete, sensory, small.
Let dialogue sting. People in real conflict interrupt, under-explain, say the almost-right thing. A line that lands badly teaches more than a paragraph about why it would.
Surprise honestly: the counter-intuitive move, the response that works for a reason the listener did not expect, the exception that overturns the obvious advice. Surprise must come from the situation, never from withheld information or a twist the setup did not support.

These raise tension, and none of them licenses invention. Do not fabricate studies, statistics, quotes, credentials, testimony or manufactured controversy; do not claim guaranteed outcomes; do not invent peril that the everyday situation would not contain; do not ridicule the character or teach coercion. Stakes come from making the real, ordinary cost vivid -- not from raising the events beyond what actually happens in a kitchen, an office or a checkout queue.

## The gap between the failure and the rematch
Episodes die in the middle. The first attempt fails early and the listener now wants one thing: to watch this person try again. Every word spent before that rematch is borrowed against their patience.
Budget it. From the end of the failed attempt to the start of the second attempt, spend at most a quarter of the episode's words. If the teaching needs more room than that, it does not go here -- it goes after the rematch, where it becomes the harder application the listener has already earned.
Cut the runway to the rematch to the minimum that makes the second attempt intelligible: the reason the first one failed, the technique in one concrete line, and the thing that now makes trying again cost more. Nothing else. Preparation montages, lists of alternative approaches and "you could also" branches belong after the payoff or nowhere.
Do not let the middle become a second explanation of the same point in different words. If two beats teach the same lesson, keep the one with dialogue in it.
