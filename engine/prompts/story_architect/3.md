## What to produce

Plan the ending first. Decide the hook, the payoff the video ends on, and one
one-tap engagement question, and only then lay out the acts that get you there.

- **Hook** — the opening claim. State something specific and surprising, and
  withhold the resolution. It is the promise the payoff has to keep.
- **Acts** — 3 to 5. Each act must make the previous one matter more. Transitions
  carry BUT or THEREFORE, never AND THEN. If an act could be cut without breaking
  the chain, it is padding: cut it and use a better one.
- **Payoff** — the line the video ends on. It must top the hook, and it must be
  the thing the hook was withholding.
- **comment_hook** — one question a viewer can answer in a single tap, woven in
  around the two-thirds mark. Not a generic "what do you think".
- **outro_line** — 8 to 16 spoken words that give a concrete reason to send the
  video to a specific person.
- **title** — at most 100 characters. Lead with the stakes and the tension; do
  not give away the resolution. This is the *narrative* title. A separate SEO
  pass writes the listing title later, from the finished narration, so write
  this one for the story rather than for search.

Do not write a description or tags. A dedicated SEO agent produces those from
the completed script, where the searchable specifics actually are.

## Why a viewer keeps watching for nine minutes, not thirty seconds

A Shorts hook opens one question and closes it in under thirty seconds — a
single spike of curiosity, done before it can fade. A nine-minute video cannot
run on one spike. It has to keep opening and closing questions the entire way
through, which means the *skeleton itself* has to be built for that, not just
the finished narration layered on top of it.

Two things make that work, and both are your job, not the script writer's:

**Never let the viewer run out of open questions.** Curiosity is a real,
trackable state: once a question is answered and nothing new has been raised,
attention has nowhere to go. Keep two or three questions open at any point in
the outline — one spanning the whole video (the hook's promise), one spanning
the current act, and the one each scene will raise and answer. An act that
resolves completely with nothing pointing forward is a dead end; make sure the
act premise below always closes on a BUT or THEREFORE that opens the next
question, never on a clean stop.

**A new question has to be built from what the viewer just learned, not
dropped in cold.** A gap that is too small is boring; a gap that assumes
context the viewer doesn't have yet reads as a non-sequitur and the curiosity
never engages at all. This is the actual risk in a multi-subject video (a
countdown, an anthology, a "five things" format): each new subject resets the
viewer's context to zero, and the video has to re-earn attention from nothing,
five times.

If your topic is naturally an anthology, you must give it a **spine** — one
rule, question, or mechanism, stated explicitly early, that every subsequent
item is shown to obey. The spine is what lets act 3 open with attention still
engaged from act 1, instead of resetting: the viewer isn't learning a new fact
from nothing, they're testing the rule they already have against a new case.
"The proof isn't plausibility, it's paperwork" is a spine. "Here's another
weird thing that happened" is not — it is five unconnected hooks wearing one
title, and it will bleed viewers at every single transition.

## retention_beats — mark where attention is most likely to leak

Produce 5 to 9 entries, each an `at_fraction` (0 to 1, position in the finished
video) and a `device` (what actually happens there, specific enough to write
from). This is not decoration — the position of every act transition is the
single highest-risk moment for a viewer to leave, because it's where an old
question just closed and, until the next line lands, none is open yet. Put a
beat at or just before every act boundary, describing the specific pivot,
reveal, or re-hook that closes the old thread and opens the new one in the same
breath. Add one beat near the middle of the video that is a genuine payoff — a
real answer landing, not another question — so the video isn't all tension and
no relief. Space the rest so no stretch longer than roughly 15% of the runtime
goes by with nothing marked.

## Constraints

- Ground every claim in something real. Embellish how a true thing is framed;
  never invent a fact, a number, or an event.
- No medical or health topics. If the brief drifts there, reframe it.
- `target_words` per act should sum to roughly 150 words per minute of the
  requested duration.

## Confidence

Report your honest self-assessment. A low score on a thin brief is more useful
than false certainty — it routes the work to a human instead of wasting a
render. Score `premise_strength` (is there a real story here), `novelty` (would
this feel fresh to someone who reads about the subject), and `visual_potential`
(can this be shown, not just narrated).
