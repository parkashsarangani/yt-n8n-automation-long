## The comprehension test

Before writing anything, decide what a viewer should be able to explain correctly *after* watching, that they could not explain *before*. Everything in this outline exists to get them there. If an act does not move the viewer closer to correctly explaining the idea, cut it — a satisfying twist that does not teach anything is the wrong kind of satisfying here.

Plan the acts as a comprehension arc, in this order:

1. **Hook**: state the real question or a moment where the concept actually matters — not "today we'll explain X." The viewer should think "wait, why DOES that happen?"
2. **Intuitive answer**: the natural, common-sense guess most people would make. It should sound reasonable, not like a strawman planted to be knocked down.
3. **Objection / the gap**: something that breaks the intuitive answer — a case it doesn't cover, a consequence that doesn't follow, a question it can't answer.
4. **Visual model / concrete example**: the thing that makes the real mechanism visible — a comparison, a physical analogy, a concrete case worked through step by step. This is where understanding actually happens; do not rush it.
5. **Correction**: state the accurate idea plainly, built directly on top of the gap the objection exposed, not as a fresh unrelated fact.
6. **Implication**: one honest consequence of the corrected idea — something it changes, predicts, or explains that the intuitive answer couldn't.
7. **Practical takeaway**: what a viewer does differently now, or what to look for, stated concretely.
8. **Recap that proves it landed**: the closing beat should show a character correctly explaining the idea back in their own words — not a narrator summary, not restating the title. If a viewer only watched this beat, they should still walk away with the corrected idea, not just a "lesson learned" platitude.

Fold these into 3-5 acts (a single act may cover more than one comprehension beat for short episodes; longer episodes can give the visual model and the implication their own acts). Every act must still change what the viewer understands, not just what's happening on screen.

## Anchor before abstraction

Open on something a viewer recognizes — a situation, an object, a decision, a moment of confusion — not a topic announcement. The abstract idea should arrive because the anchor demands it, not the other way around.

Examples of good anchors:
- a decision that goes wrong for a reason nobody expected;
- an everyday object that only makes sense once you know the mechanism;
- a moment where the "obvious" answer clearly fails;
- a number or outcome that looks wrong until the real cause is shown.

## Structure by requested duration

Honor the requested duration. `target_words` across all acts should total roughly 150 words per requested minute.

- Up to ~90 seconds: 3 compact acts. Compress intuitive-answer/objection into one act and correction/implication into another; keep the recap as its own beat even when short.
- ~2-5 minutes: 3-4 acts, enough room to let the visual model actually land before moving on.
- Longer episodes: 4-5 acts. Do not add filler acts — if the idea doesn't need more room, keep the episode short.

Do not pad short episodes to imitate long-form. Do not compress a genuinely complex idea into a short explainer where the visual model has no room to work.

## Story requirements

- Hook: specific, connected to the anchor, and it must be a real question the episode actually answers — never a question the episode later ignores.
- Acts: each must change what the viewer understands, not merely what happens. Transitions carry BUT or THEREFORE, not a loose AND THEN.
- Payoff: the corrected idea stated through the recap beat, landing harder than the opening's intuitive answer — a viewer should notice the gap between what they assumed and what they now know.
- `comment_hook`: a one-tap question that invites viewers to test the corrected idea against their own experience, not a generic "what do you think?"
- `outro_line`: 8-16 words giving a concrete reason to share with a specific kind of person who has the intuitive-answer misconception right now.
- Title: <=100 characters, states or implies the gap between intuition and reality, no spoiler of the correction itself.

For anthology-like topics, create a spine: one mechanism or question that every example tests. Do not reset context from zero for every item.

## Facts and safety

Ground claims in reality. Never invent a statistic, event, mechanism, law, or quote. The intuitive answer must be a genuine, common misconception — not a fabricated one invented just to be wrong.

Everyday emotions, habits, and social behavior are allowed as ordinary human experiences. Do not diagnose viewers, claim they have a disorder, prescribe treatment, or make medical promises.

## Confidence

Score exactly:
- `premise_strength`
- `novelty`
- `visual_potential`

`premise_strength` should be low if the outline is just an abstract explanation with no concrete anchor a viewer can recognize, or if the intuitive answer / objection / correction arc is missing or too thin to actually teach anything.
