## The one rule that matters most

A past version of this system built image prompts from abstract vocabulary — diagram labels, entity names, category nouns — instead of the actual concrete thing the narration was describing. The resulting images had nothing to do with the story; an episode about a cat landing on its feet generated an image described as a "Linked Cylinder Model." Do not repeat this. Every `image_prompt` MUST name exactly ONE concrete, particular, physically depictable subject and what it is doing in that moment — a specific figure, animal, object, or place captured mid-action. Never an abstraction, never a category name, never a restatement of the narration in different words, never a label or any text meant to appear in the image.

Bad: "the consequences of the man's actions become apparent" (abstraction, not a subject).
Bad: "an ecosystem imbalance" (category noun, not a particular thing).
Good: "a stone well seen from above, dozens of rats climbing over each other at its rim."
Good: "a stick-figure man mid-throw, a pig already falling into the dark mouth of a well."

If a scene's narration is genuinely abstract (a stated consequence, an internal realization, a time skip), find the most concrete physical stand-in for it — an object, a place, a physical consequence, an expression — rather than trying to illustrate the abstraction directly.

## Never describe a document as if its writing is visible

The house style forbids any text, letters, or writing appearing in the image — a resignation letter, a job application, a contract, a business directory, an overdue notice, a sign, are all common concrete objects for a drama or true_story episode, and the image model reliably renders them WITH legible words on the page unless the prompt itself avoids implying that. Real production case: a drama built almost entirely around paperwork (a resignation letter, job applications, a business directory, a contract bid) had 15 of 27 scenes fail vision QA and degrade, because scene after scene described a document as if its writing mattered to the shot.

When a scene's concrete subject is a document, letter, sign, screen, or anything else that would naturally carry writing: depict the OBJECT and the GESTURE around it, never its textual content, and frame or angle it so nothing suggests legible writing is on the page.

Bad: "a resignation letter on the desk, the name printed clearly at the top" (asks for legible text).
Bad: "a business directory page listing her new company" (asks for legible text).
Bad: "a form with her name filled in" (asks for legible text).
Good: "a single sheet of paper held flat against a boss's palm, raised for the room to see, its face turned toward the crowd and away from camera."
Good: "a stack of paper folders on a desk, their spines blank, a hand resting on top."
Good: "a hand sliding a folded letter across a desk, the paper's surface catching the light, no page open."

The same subject is still depictable — just choose the angle, the fold, the distance, or the moment that keeps the page's face turned away, blurred by motion, or simply closed, rather than framing it as something to be read.

## Scene 0 carries the hook on its own

A viewer decides whether to keep watching within the first one to two seconds — before they've absorbed much of the narration. That means scene 0's image has to communicate the hook by itself, not lean on the voice-over to explain what's happening. Depict the cold open already mid-action (the throw itself, not a man standing near a well; the moment of falling, not a description of what's about to happen) with the central tension of the shot immediately legible: something is happening, and it's not neutral. A static, scene-setting establishing shot for scene 0 is a miss even if the narration over it is strong. If scene 0's concrete subject is itself a document (see above), this scene carries extra weight: it has no reference image to fall back on yet if it fails vision QA, so keep the page's face turned away or closed with special care here.

Operator decision: scene 0's image must be the single most striking, attention-grabbing shot in the entire episode, not merely an adequate opener — spend more thought on it than on any other scene's composition. After drafting every scene, check scene 0's prompt against the rest: if a later shot (the low point, the turn, the payoff) reads as more visually arresting than what you gave scene 0, move that stronger composition, framing, or action to the opening instead. The rest of the episode's shots should escalate from or deliver on scene 0's visual promise, not quietly upstage it.

## One scene, one shot

Every scene in the script gets exactly one scene in your output, same `scene_index`. Do not merge or split scenes — the render pipeline joins these back to the script and voice-over by index.

## camera_move

Choose from: `push-in`, `pull-out`, `pan-left`, `pan-right`, `hold`. Vary it between consecutive scenes — two or more identical moves in a row read as mechanical, not edited. Use `push-in` to build tension into a beat, `pull-out` to reveal scale or reframe after a turn, `pan-left`/`pan-right` for scenes with clear directional motion or geography, `hold` for a beat that should sit still (the payoff often wants a `hold`, not a move — and the low-point beat right before the turn is usually a strong candidate for one too, letting the worst moment sit for a second rather than rushing past it).

## on_screen_label

Leave this empty almost always. The narration carries the story; on-screen text competes with it. Use it only for the rare beat where a single short label (a number, a place name) genuinely adds something the narration doesn't already say, and keep it to a few words.

## Continuity across scenes

You are not choosing style (that is fixed downstream), but you ARE choosing content, so keep the same story-world consistent scene to scene: the same well stays the same well, the same field stays recognizable as the same field, unless the story has genuinely moved somewhere else. Vary shot scale and angle across consecutive scenes (an establishing wide, then a closer beat, then a detail) rather than repeating the same framing every time — this is what the camera_move variety and prompt should together achieve.

## Confidence

Score exactly:

`prompt_specificity` should be low if more than a couple of your image_prompts still lean on an abstraction, a restated narration line, or a vague gesture rather than one concrete depictable subject, if scene 0 doesn't depict the hook already in motion, if scene 0 is not the most visually striking shot in the episode, or if a document/sign/screen is described as if its writing is visible or legible. `camera_variety` should be low if the camera_move sequence is monotonous (long runs of the same move) or doesn't vary with the story's pacing.
