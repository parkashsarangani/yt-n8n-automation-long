## Give it a story, not a topic

Before writing a single line, decide: **why is this conversation happening
right now, and what does each character want out of it?** A pair of
characters who are simply "discussing" the topic — trading facts back and
forth because it's their turn — reads as random dialogue, not a story, no
matter how accurate or well-paced the facts are. That is the single most
common way this fails.

Invent a light narrative frame that puts the cast INSIDE the topic instead
of beside it. It does not need to be an elaborate plot — it needs a reason
the stakes feel real for these two characters, right now. Cheap, reliable
frames:

- A disagreement or bet that the facts will settle one way or the other.
- One character trying to prove something to the other, who keeps pushing
  back.
- A mystery or "why would that even be true" that they're working out
  together, in real time, not reporting after the fact.
- A problem the topic is standing in the way of (they need to get
  somewhere, win an argument, avoid a consequence) that the facts turn out
  to matter for.
- Something happening TO them right now (they just discovered this, walked
  into this, are about to be proven wrong about this) that they're reacting
  to live.

Every fact from the story skeleton must surface because a character needs
it, doubts it, is trying to use it, or is blindsided by it — never because
it is "the next interesting thing to mention." If a line could be deleted
and swapped for any other fact from the topic with zero effect on the
characters or the stakes, it is not doing its job yet.

## What to produce

A flat list of scenes covering every act in order. Each scene is ONE spoken
LINE, not a paragraph: one character says one thing, roughly 8 to 20 words,
before the next line — same speaker continuing a thought, or a different
speaker responding — takes over as the next scene.

- Number `scene_index` continuously from 0 across the whole video, and set
  `act_index` to the act each scene belongs to.
- `speaker` — the `character_id` who says this line.
- `emotion` — how they say it: `neutral`, `happy`, `angry`, `surprised`,
  `sad`, or `scared`. This drives the renderer's expression and pose, not
  just flavor text — pick the one that's actually true of the line, and
  vary it scene to scene the way a real exchange would.
- `narration` — the line itself, exactly as spoken. Dialogue only. Never
  write stage directions, action descriptions, or third-person narration in
  this field — if it wouldn't come out of the character's mouth, it doesn't
  belong here.
- `point` — what this beat does for the story (one short phrase, for the
  editor — it is never spoken).
- Hit each act's `target_words` within about 10%, counting every
  character's lines together.

## End every act on a real cliffhanger

Not just the video overall — every single act break. The last line or two
of an act must leave something genuinely unresolved: a threat not yet
dealt with, a reveal cut off half-said, a decision about to be made, a
question whose answer would change what the viewer thinks they know. "That
covers this point, on to the next one" is not a cliffhanger even if the
next act is more interesting — it is a scene change, and it reads as one.

A cliffhanger raises a NEW question or danger; it does not just leave the
current one unanswered by accident. If you can't state, in one sentence,
what the viewer is now worried about or dying to know, the act break isn't
working yet.

Stakes should escalate act to act, not stay flat. The thing at risk, in
doubt, or in play in the final act should feel bigger or more urgent than
what opened the first one — even if the underlying topic is the same
throughout.

## Honor retention_beats — this is not optional

The story carries a `retention_beats` array: fractional positions in the
video (`at_fraction`, 0 to 1) and what should happen there (`device`).
These were placed deliberately at the moments most likely to lose a viewer.
Convert each `at_fraction` into an approximate line position the same way
you'd do it for prose (running word count through the acts), find the line
nearest each beat, and write it — and the exchange around it — to actually
perform the named device. A single line rarely carries a whole retention
beat on its own; let two or three lines of back-and-forth build to it.

## Keep a question open at all times

Never let the dialogue reach a point where the last line fully resolved
something and nothing new is pulling forward. Hold two or three threads
open at once: one spanning the whole video (tied to the story frame you
invented above, not just the topic), one spanning the current act, and a
small one inside whatever exchange is playing. A character can BE the open
question — one of them not knowing something the other does, or being
wrong about something the audience can tell is about to bite them, is one
of the cheapest, most natural ways to keep dialogue pulling forward.

## Writing dialogue that sounds like people, not narration

- Real back-and-forth. Characters interrupt, react, ask the question the
  audience is thinking, get it wrong before getting it right, push back on
  each other. Two lines that could be swapped between speakers with no
  change are a sign nobody has a voice yet.
- Give each character a consistent voice matching their cast
  `personality` — the skeptic stays skeptical, the enthusiast stays
  enthusiastic, all the way through, not just in their first scene.
- A run of many consecutive lines from one speaker reads as a lecture, not
  a cartoon. Cut back in the other character(s) — a reaction, a question,
  a one-word interjection — well before that happens.
- Humor comes from character and from the facts themselves, not from
  narrator asides — there is no narrator anymore.
- Short lines. This is spoken aloud by an animated puppet, not read. Avoid
  semicolons and long subordinate clauses.

Weave the `comment_hook` question into the dialogue naturally at around the
two-thirds mark — one character can genuinely ask it, or wonder it aloud.
End the final scene on the payoff, delivered as a line that pays off the
story frame you invented, not a summary of facts.

## Constraints

- Never state a fact the story skeleton does not support. If you need a
  detail you do not have, write around it.
- No medical or health content.
- Do not write an outro scene; it is appended downstream.

## Confidence

Score `narrative_coherence`, `pacing`, `hook_strength`, and
`retention_fidelity` as before, plus `cast_fidelity` (does every character
sound distinct and consistent with their cast `personality` from their
first line to their last) and `story_frame_strength` (is there an actual
situation with stakes the characters are in, that a viewer could describe
in one sentence — or does this read as two characters taking turns stating
facts about the topic). Be honest — a low score routes this to a human
rather than to a render.
