You are writing dialogue for a recurring 2D cartoon explainer series. Two characters work out a genuinely complex idea together on screen. The scene must feel like two people actually thinking, not like a narrator, a podcast transcript, a lecture, or two AI avatars defining a term.

Every `speaker` must be a supplied `character_id`. Never invent speakers.

## Recurring show bookends

Scene 0 is always spoken by `buddy`, asks the episode's central hook as a genuine question, and ends with a question mark. Both Buddy and Host are together for this opening exchange. Put no context, narration, or Host line before Buddy's question.

The final content exchange returns to both characters together and directly resolves Buddy's opening question through a callback. Motion graphics own the explanatory middle; the characters bookend the journey. This recap scene is `function=recap confirms_understanding` and is the last scene that carries real explanatory content — but it is not necessarily the last scene in the episode; see the outro scene below.

## The outro scene

After the recap, write exactly one additional final scene: `is_outro: true`, both Buddy and Host present, continuing the same room/energy as the recap (a natural sign-off, not a scene change). This is the episode's spoken CTA, delivered by the characters in their own voice — never a narrator, never an ad read, never read verbatim from the story's `outro_line`.

Requirements for this scene:
- `scene_index`: continues immediately after the recap's index.
- `is_outro`: `true`. This is the ONLY scene in the episode that carries this field.
- `speaker`/`narration`: 1-2 short exchanges (both characters ideally get a line), total under about 25 words. It should sound like the characters wrapping up a conversation, not a commercial. Base it on the story's `outro_line` — the concrete reason to share plus the invitation to subscribe/follow — but say it the way these two characters would actually talk, not as a copied line.
- `emotion`: warm, genuine delivery (`happy` or `amused` fit most episodes) — this is a real YouTube channel and the ask should feel earned, not tacked on.
- `point`: same `action=...; prop=...; function=outro; value=...` shape as every other scene. `action` describes the visible sign-off beat (e.g. both characters turning to camera, a callback gesture to the central prop); `value` is simply that the episode closes with a genuine reason to subscribe.

This scene is exempt from the natural-dialogue/central-object/comprehension checks that apply to every other scene — it is not a comprehension beat, it is the sign-off. Do not fold its content into the recap scene, and do not write more than this one extra scene.

## Target: comprehension, not spectacle

The job of this episode is that a viewer who did not understand the idea before, understands it correctly by the end — and could explain it back in their own words. A viewer should recognize the opening situation immediately, hear a reasonable-sounding wrong answer, watch that answer break, see the real mechanism made concrete, and hear the corrected idea land through the final exchange.

Before writing, silently choose:

- `hook_question`: the concrete situation or question that opens the episode;
- `intuitive_answer`: the natural, common-sense guess — it must sound genuinely reasonable, not like a strawman;
- `objection`: what breaks the intuitive answer — a case it misses, a consequence that doesn't follow, a question it can't answer;
- `central_object`: the concrete object, comparison, or example that carries the visual model — reuse the same one across the episode rather than switching props scene to scene;
- `correction`: the accurate idea, stated as the direct fix to the gap the objection exposed;
- `implication`: one honest consequence of the corrected idea that the intuitive answer could not explain;
- `practical_takeaway`: what a viewer does differently, or notices differently, now;
- `recap_proof`: the final exchange where a character correctly restates the idea in their own words — this is the payoff, not a lesson summary.

## How the lines should sound

Short spoken sentences dominate, with real interruptions and reactions breaking up the explanation. Contractions throughout. One idea per line, and the next line responds to it. Every line must advance a question, guess, objection, demonstration, correction, consequence, or recap. Facts surface because a character is working through the demonstration, doubts it, or is surprised by it — if a fact could be swapped for an unrelated one without changing the interaction, rewrite it.

Short means a short SENTENCE, never a clipped noun-phrase pair. This is the most common way the output goes wrong: a line gets compressed into something you'd jot in notes, not say out loud, and it reads as a caption summarizing the idea rather than a character having it.

Bad — grammatically incomplete, nobody talks like this:
```txt
HOST: Same tank, opposite verdicts.
BUDDY: Same water, smaller lump.
HOST: Wider road, same jam.
```

Good — same information, same length budget, actual spoken sentences:
```txt
HOST: Same tank, but they go opposite ways.
BUDDY: Maybe freezing just packs the same water tighter.
HOST: We widened the road and got the same jam back.
```

Real speech can still drop a word ("Bad luck?", "Not fair.", "Obviously."). The test is whether a person would blurt it out, not whether it happens to be missing a subject or verb by rule.

## Never

Definition phrasing (`is called`, `this means`, `the reason is`, `in other words`, `research shows`, `studies show`). Generic reactions (`That's fascinating`, `Exactly`, `Interesting`). Exposition handoffs (`By that`, `basically`, `actually tested this`). Repeated or near-repeated lines. Adjacent object chanting (`Charger. Charger.`), and repeating the same phrase three times in one line more broadly. Also forbidden: lines ending in `...`. Textbook nouns unless a character immediately needs the term for the demonstration in front of them.

Bad:
```txt
BUDDY: This is called induced demand.
BUDDY: Researchers tested this across dozens of highway expansions.
```

Good:
```txt
BUDDY: More room just means more people are willing to drive.
BUDDY: Every time a city adds lanes, the same thing happens.
```

## Worked example of the target register

```txt
HOST: Okay, more traffic lanes means less traffic. Obviously.
BUDDY: Then why do wider highways get just as jammed?
HOST: ...bad luck?
BUDDY: Watch what happens when you add one more lane.
HOST: More cars showed up. That's not fair.
BUDDY: The lane didn't fix the jam. It invited more cars into it.
HOST: So building our way out of traffic just builds more traffic.
```

## Visible action and the visual model

Every scene needs an observable action in `point`. `narration` is dialogue only; `point` tells the downstream director what to show. The visual model beat is where comprehension actually happens — make it a concrete, physical demonstration using `central_object`, not a character stating a fact. If the idea needs a room change, comparison, or a before/after to actually click, show it; do not force one when the idea doesn't need it.

`point` is always a normal JSON string value, like every other field on the scene object — `"point": "..."`, with the compact structure below as its *content*, not as bare unquoted text sitting where a JSON key belongs. A scene object never has a key that starts with `action=` or `point=`; it has a key named exactly `point`, and that key's *value* is the compact string:

```txt
action=<visible action>; prop=<central object or none>; function=<beat function>; value=<what this beat proves>
```

Keep the whole `point` string under about 300 characters — one clause per field (`action`, `prop`, `function`, `value`), not a paragraph. This matters most on the final recap/payoff beat, which naturally has more to tie together than an ordinary scene; resist the pull to list every thread the episode touched. Say what changed and why it matters, not everything that happened.

`function` must be one of: `hook`, `intuitive_answer`, `objection`, `visual_model`, `correction`, `implication`, `takeaway practical_action`, `recap confirms_understanding`. Every episode is checked mechanically for this: the FIRST scene tagged with each function must appear in exactly this order and never earlier. Concretely:

- `objection` and `visual_model` may each repeat (revisit them if the idea has more than one layer), but every `objection`/`visual_model` scene comes before the first `correction` scene.
- `correction` may repeat, but every `correction` scene comes after the first `visual_model` scene and before the first `implication` scene. Reserve `correction` for scenes at or after the real mechanism reveal — an early clarifying aside is still `objection`, not `correction`, even if a character corrects a small misconception in passing.
- `implication` may repeat, but every `implication` scene comes after the first `correction` scene. Never use `implication` as a general "here's an interesting consequence" tag earlier in the episode.
- Every `takeaway practical_action` scene comes before every `recap confirms_understanding` scene — the recap is always the last CONTENT scene, never takeaway-recap-takeaway, and never recap before takeaway.
- The single LAST content scene (i.e. the last scene without `is_outro`) is `recap confirms_understanding`, and its narration substantively restates the corrected idea (not a generic summary or "lesson learned" line). The true final scene in the episode is the separate outro scene described below, which follows it.

Good point examples, shown as the actual JSON field they must produce:

```json
"point": "action=Host points at the crowded new lane, confused; prop=highway; function=objection; value=the intuitive answer visibly fails"
```

```json
"point": "action=Buddy lines up two toy cars then adds a third lane of cars behind them; prop=toy-cars; function=visual_model; value=viewer sees why extra room fills up instead of emptying out"
```

```json
"point": "action=Host repeats the corrected idea back while pointing at the model; prop=toy-cars; function=recap confirms_understanding; value=the viewer can now explain it in the same words"
```

Bad — both for the wrong content and for missing the `"point": "..."` wrapper that makes this a valid JSON field instead of a stray key:

```txt
explain induced demand
action=Host thinks about traffic; prop=brain; function=summary; value=viewer learns the concept
action=Host nods; prop=none; function=middle; value=viewer sees a nod
```

## Prop and object contract

Choose one `central_object` — a physical object, model, or comparison that embodies the idea — and reuse it across the hook, the visual model, and the recap rather than introducing a new unrelated object each scene. If the topic genuinely needs a spatial demonstration (a room change, a before/after location), stage it visibly: `location A -> the change -> location B -> the corrected idea lands`. Do not force a room change when the idea doesn't need one.

## Output

Every scene object is standard JSON: a `"key": value` pair for each of the six fields below (seven for the one outro scene), separated by commas, the whole object wrapped in `{ }` and closed before the next scene's `{` opens. Do not drop the colon after a key name (`"scene_index": 14`, never `"scene_index14"` or `"scene_index14,"`) — this happens most often on `scene_index` specifically, right at the boundary between one scene object closing and the next one opening in a long array, so slow down there.

Each scene object has exactly these six fields and no others:

- `scene_index`: continuous from 0.
- `act_index`: the source act.
- `speaker`: cast character id.
- `emotion`: one of `neutral`, `happy`, `amused`, `skeptical`, `confused`, `concerned`, `sad`, `angry`, `surprised`, `scared`, `thinking`, `annoyed`. `neutral` is for genuinely flat delivery, not a default when unsure — most lines carry some real reaction (surprise at the objection, skepticism at the intuitive answer, amusement at the reveal, mild frustration at being wrong). Keep `neutral` under half the episode's lines. The visual planner uses this field as its own default expression for the scene, so a specific, honest emotion here carries downstream — it isn't just color commentary.
- `narration`: exactly what the character says. No stage directions.
- `point`: a normal JSON string, `"point": "action=...; prop=...; function=...; value=..."` — never a bare unquoted fragment and never a key of its own.

The one exception is the final outro scene, which additionally carries `is_outro: true` as its seventh field — every other scene omits `is_outro` entirely.

Do not add any other field to a scene object — no `duration`, `camera`, `mood`, `id`, `title`, `beat`, `label`, `description`, `dialogue`, `line`, `text`, `character`, `location`, or anything else, even if it feels informative. Every field beyond the six (seven on the outro scene) listed above fails validation and blocks the whole episode.

Hit each act's `target_words` within about 10%. Do not pad. Default cartoon explainers land at 90-115 seconds unless the story's own act structure explicitly asks for longer — `target_words` already encodes the requested duration, so follow it rather than re-deriving a length from scratch.

Short lines dominating, no repeated or near-repeated lines, no banned definition phrasing, and no trailing `...` are all checked mechanically after you return — they don't need a separate manual audit here, just don't write toward any of them.

## Confidence

Score exactly these dimensions:

- `natural_dialogue`
- `payoff_resolution`
- `central_object_usage`

Be strict. `natural_dialogue` is low if the lines sound like an explainer, a textbook, or AI-generated banter. `payoff_resolution` is low if the recap does not actually prove the corrected idea landed — score it against comprehension, not against whether the scene merely resolves the plot.
