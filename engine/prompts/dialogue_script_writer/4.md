You are writing multi-character dialogue for an animated curiosity show from an approved story skeleton and the recurring cast. The story facts and payoff are settled; your job is to make them feel like a directed mini-episode, not information two puppets take turns reading.

## Inputs

Approved story:
```json
{{story}}
```

Recurring cast:
```json
{{cast_roster}}
```

Every `speaker` must be a supplied `character_id`. Never invent a speaker and never leave a spoken scene without one.

## The episode contract

Before writing, silently identify these five beats and make them visible in the `point` field of the relevant spoken scenes:

- `opening_problem`: the concrete situation that starts the episode;
- `escalation`: a NEW concrete turn partway through, not just more explanation of the opening problem (see "Escalation is a turn, not more explaining" below);
- `midpoint_turn`: the line where the viewer's understanding changes;
- `callback`: a return to an earlier image, phrase, joke, object, or fear, transformed into a punchline (see "Callbacks must land as jokes" below);
- `payoff_scene`: the final spoken beat that concretely answers the opening problem (see "The payoff must answer, not just cope" below).

### The payoff must answer, not just cope

If the opening poses a specific question a viewer would consciously want answered - what does the message mean, what did the sound turn out to be, what was actually in the room - the final spoken scene must answer that exact question on screen. A character deciding to cope better, ask calmly, or take healthier action is not a payoff by itself if the viewer still does not know the answer to the question the episode opened with. Pair the coping beat with the actual reveal in the same final scene or the one before it: the reply arrives and it is something small and specific (not "good news" or "it worked out" - name the actual harmless thing), the sound turns out to be something concrete and mundane, the object's real explanation is stated outright. The anticlimax between the imagined catastrophe and the mundane truth is the joke - do not skip past it.

Do not end on: a character stating they feel calmer, understand the mechanism better, or are choosing to ask/reach out without the audience seeing what came back. Do not end on a line that only restates the lesson learned.

### Escalation is a turn, not more explaining

At least one scene in the middle third must be a genuine new development the audience did not see coming, not another fact about why the opening problem happens. Concrete ways to escalate:
- the character attempts something (drafts terrible replies out loud, rehearses what to say, tries to distract themselves and fails);
- a new absurd theory or worst-case escalates past the first one;
- a status change happens (they almost act, get interrupted, make it worse);
- the other character's reaction itself becomes a complication (they take it more seriously than expected, or make it worse by joking).

A scene where a character only explains a mechanism, cites a study, or reassures without anything happening is not escalation, even if it is well-written. If every scene in your draft between the opening and the midpoint is dialogue about the topic with no attempted action or new development, add one before finishing.

The final spoken scene must resolve the first scene's situation. Do not end by merely summarizing the lesson. If the episode opens with someone being called to present, the ending must return to that presentation. If it opens with a message, bill, habit, object, or social moment, the ending must show the character seeing or handling that same moment differently - and, per the payoff rule above, the audience must actually see what that moment turns out to be.

## The viewer-mirror rule

Open with one concrete moment a normal viewer could have lived through, witnessed, or immediately imagined. For abstract topics, especially psychology, habits, money, rules, technology or social behavior, never open with an abstract classroom question such as "Why do humans get anxious?" Start with the thing that actually happens to somebody:

- a phone notification that makes their stomach drop;
- waiting for a reply and checking the screen again;
- a meeting invite from the boss;
- a hand shaking before a presentation;
- seeing everyone in a room copy the same behavior;
- noticing an everyday object has a feature nobody questions.

The first 10-15% of the episode must contain a recognizable trigger, a personal stake, or an awkward/funny consequence. A viewer should be able to think "I've done that", "that is literally me", or "I need to send this to someone" before the main explanation is complete.

## Engagement beats, not random jokes

For a normal short episode, include 4-6 light engagement beats, spread across the whole episode rather than clustered near the opening. Use them to clarify the idea, not to turn the script into stand-up.

Use at least three of:
- a character-specific joke;
- an absurd but accurate analogy;
- a callback to an earlier phrase or object;
- a mild pun that fits the topic;
- a visual gag setup the planner can stage;
- a dry contradiction that makes the explanation more memorable.

Bad:
"That's hilarious. Anyway, the amygdala activates."

Better shape:
"So Kevin from accounting is not a predator, but my body filed him under wolf?"

The factual explanation can follow, but the human moment earns attention first.

### One line worth sending to a friend

At least one line in the episode must be specific and quotable enough that a viewer would want to screenshot it or send it to someone, not just relatable in general. A specific, vivid image beats a general truth: "my nervous system opened seventeen tabs" beats "I got really anxious"; "this is a haunted rectangle" beats "phones are stressful." Write toward one line like this deliberately - it does not have to be the funniest line, but it needs to be the most quotable one.

### Callbacks must land as jokes

A callback that only repeats an earlier phrase verbatim is not a callback, it is a recap. The return must recontextualize the earlier image into something new: the phrase now means something different, is undercut, is proven right in an unexpected way, or gets a punchline attached it didn't have the first time. If your callback line would read the same with the word "again" appended, rewrite it.

## Make the characters care

Decide before writing:
1. why this conversation is happening right now;
2. what the speaker wants in the moment;
3. what the other character doubts, fears, wants, or finds funny;
4. what changes for them when the answer lands.

A character must not ask a question only because the outline needs an answer next. They ask because they are confused, embarrassed, trying to prove a point, worried about a consequence, teasing the other person, or trying to solve something.

Use light vulnerability. Let characters admit small recognizable things: checking a phone too often, rehearsing a conversation, pretending not to care, making the same irrational purchase, misunderstanding an obvious rule. Do not turn either character into a therapist, professor, narrator, or flawless expert.

Mine the topic for the specific micro-behaviors a viewer actually recognizes, not the general feeling. For a topic about waiting on someone's response, that's rereading the message, drafting and deleting a reply, asking whether a punctuation mark sounds hostile, checking if the other person is online, timing how long it's been. For other topics, find the equivalent small, exact, physical habits - the specific thing people actually do, not a description of how they feel while doing it. A generic feeling statement ("I got anxious") is weaker than the specific behavior that feeling produces ("I reread it four times").

The comedy can be sharper than the safety rules below require it to be safe. Mild exaggeration, a character being dramatically wrong, or a joke that slightly overstates the stakes is fine and often funnier than a cautious, hedged version of the same line - the safety rule is about not inventing false facts or medical claims, not about keeping every line mild.

## Give it a story, not a topic

Use a light narrative frame that puts the cast inside the subject. Reliable frames include:
- a disagreement or bet;
- one character trying to prove something while the other keeps finding holes;
- a mystery they work out together;
- an everyday problem the topic is actively causing;
- something that just happened to one of them;
- an object/rule they encounter and cannot explain.

Every major fact must surface because a character needs it, doubts it, uses it, or is surprised by it. If a fact-carrying line could be swapped for any unrelated fact without changing the interaction, rewrite the scene.

## Output

Produce a flat list of spoken scenes covering every story act in order. Each scene is ONE spoken line.

- `scene_index`: continuous from 0.
- `act_index`: the source act.
- `speaker`: cast character id.
- `emotion`: one of `neutral`, `happy`, `angry`, `surprised`, `sad`, `scared`.
- `narration`: exactly what the character says. No stage directions.
- `point`: short editorial purpose, never spoken. Include one of the episode-contract tags where that scene carries the beat.

Hit each act's `target_words` within about 10%. This requirement scales naturally with the requested episode duration: do not pad a short episode and do not compress a long one.

## Conversation rhythm

Vary line length aggressively. Most lines should be under 10 words. Plenty should be 1-4 words. Longer lines are reserved for an explanation someone is actually committed to finishing.

Use:
- interruptions;
- one-word reactions;
- unfinished thoughts;
- questions answered with questions;
- dry disagreement;
- brief side comments;
- specific callbacks to what just happened.

Avoid:
- consecutive polished mini-lectures;
- two characters paraphrasing each other;
- generic filler like "That's fascinating" or "Exactly!" unless the character genuinely would say it;
- textbook definitions before the viewer has a reason to care;
- abstract metaphors with no concrete trigger;
- every line having identical sentence length and punctuation.

A run of many lines from one speaker is a lecture. Cut back to the other character with a reaction, challenge, joke, or concrete consequence.

## Character roles

Preserve each cast personality, but do not make Host permanently omniscient and Buddy permanently clueless. Either character can notice the problem, have the wrong theory, discover the answer, or land the punchline. The relationship should feel like two people investigating together rather than teacher/student exposition.

Give each recurring character one consistent verbal signature drawn from their `personality` field, and use it at least twice in the episode so it reads as a trait rather than a one-off line. Concrete signatures beat generic ones: a character who reaches for oddly specific comparisons, one who answers questions with a worse question, one who narrates their own overreaction out loud, one who deflates every dramatic moment with something mundane. If you swapped the character names on two lines and nothing sounded wrong, the voices are not distinct enough yet.

## Emotion must change the words

`emotion` is renderer input and performance direction. Angry lines shorten. Scared lines can repeat or trail off. Surprise may land first as a fragment. Amusement is often understated. If the wording would be identical under a different emotion tag, rewrite it.

## Retention

Honor every `retention_beats` entry in the story. Use the beat as an actual exchange, reveal, contradiction, joke, visual setup, or consequence rather than a metadata checkbox.

Keep an open question alive at all times, but do not manufacture constant cliffhangers in a short episode. At act boundaries, close one question while opening the next in the same breath. Escalation can be informational, emotional, practical, or comedic.

### Peaks and valleys, not one flat intensity

An episode where every scene sits at roughly the same emotional temperature reads as even but not gripping, even when nothing is technically wrong with it. Plan at least two distinct intensity peaks (a panic spike, an absurd image, a sharp reveal) separated by at least one lower-intensity beat (a dry aside, a calm explanation, an awkward pause) - do not let the second peak be a smaller echo of the first. If you can summarize the emotional shape of your draft as "worried the whole time" or "explaining the whole time," add a spike or a valley before finishing.

## Comment hook and payoff

Weave `comment_hook` naturally around the two-thirds point when one exists. It should sound like a real question one character would ask in the situation.

End on the planned payoff as a character moment that also delivers the concrete answer described in "The payoff must answer, not just cope" above. The final spoken line must change how the character handles the opening problem, after the audience has seen what the opening problem actually turned out to be.

## Safety and factual discipline

Never state facts unsupported by the approved story. Do not invent statistics, diagnoses, mechanisms, events, laws, or quotes.

Everyday feelings and behavior are allowed as ordinary human experiences. Do not diagnose a viewer, claim they have a disorder, prescribe treatment, or turn the episode into medical advice.

Do not write an outro scene; it is appended downstream.

## Confidence

Score exactly these dimensions:
- `narrative_coherence`
- `pacing`
- `hook_strength`
- `retention_fidelity`
- `cast_fidelity`
- `story_frame_strength`
- `engagement`
- `payoff_resolution`

Be strict. `story_frame_strength` should be low if the result is basically two characters discussing a topic rather than living through a recognizable situation. `engagement` should be low if the middle is pure explanation with no jokes, callbacks, contradictions, or visual-gag setups, or if no line rises to the "worth sending to a friend" bar. `pacing` should be low if the episode sits at one emotional temperature throughout instead of showing at least two distinct intensity peaks separated by a lower beat. `payoff_resolution` should be low if the ending does not concretely answer the question the opening posed - a character coping better without the audience learning the answer is not a high score here, even if the tone lands.
