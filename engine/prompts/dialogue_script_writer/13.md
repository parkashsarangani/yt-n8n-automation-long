## Recurring show bookends

Scene 0 is always spoken by `buddy`, asks the episode's central hook as a genuine question, and ends with a question mark. Both Buddy and Host are together for this opening exchange. Put no context, narration, or Host line before Buddy's question.

The final content exchange returns to both characters together and directly resolves Buddy's opening question through a callback. Motion graphics own the explanatory middle; the characters bookend the journey.

## Target: comprehension, not spectacle

The job of this episode is that a viewer who did not understand the idea before, understands it correctly by the end — and could explain it back in their own words. A viewer should recognize the opening situation immediately, hear a reasonable-sounding wrong answer, watch that answer break, see the real mechanism made concrete, and hear the corrected idea land through the final exchange.

Before writing, silently choose:

- `hook_question`: the concrete situation or question that opens the episode;
- `intuitive_answer`: the natural, common-sense guess — it must sound genuinely reasonable, not like a strawman;
- `objection`: what breaks the intuitive answer — a case it misses, a consequence that doesn't follow, a question it can't answer;
- `central_object`: the concrete object, comparison, or example that carries the visual model — reuse the same one across the episode rather than switching props scene to scene;
- `correction`: the accurate idea, stated as the direct fix to the gap the objection exposed;
- `implication`: one honest consequence of the corrected idea that the intuitive answer could not explain;
- `practical_takeaway`: what a viewer does differently, or notices differently, now;
- `recap_proof`: the final exchange where a character correctly restates the idea in their own words — this is the payoff, not a lesson summary.

## 9.5-level dialogue rules

Required:

- At least half of all spoken lines must be 10 words or fewer -- but short means a short SENTENCE, never a clipped noun-phrase pair. If you can't tell whether a line has a subject and a verb, it's not a sentence yet; write the version a person would actually say out loud, even if that costs a couple more words.
- At least one short interruption every 5-7 lines: a real reactive utterance a person actually says out loud -- "...bad luck?", "Wait, what?", "That's not fair." -- never a telegraphic label standing in for a sentence.
- Use contractions and ordinary speech: `I'm`, `you're`, `that's`, `don't`, `can't`, `it's`.
- Use one idea per line. Let the next line respond to it. One idea per line still means a complete spoken sentence about that one idea, not the idea's headline.
- Keep facts conversational and motivated by what the character is working through, not recited.
- Make every line advance one of these: question, guess, objection, demonstration, correction, consequence, or recap.
- Give the final line a clean recap that proves understanding, not a summary sentence.

Forbidden:

- exact repeated caption lines anywhere in the episode;
- near-duplicate lines within five scenes;
- repeating the same phrase opening three or more times;
- adjacent object chanting: `Charger. Charger.`, `Keys. Keys.`, `Phone. Phone.`;
- repeating the same phrase three times in one line;
- lines ending in `...`;
- definition phrasing: `is called`, `this means`, `the reason is`, `in other words`, `research shows`, `studies show`;
- exposition handoff phrases: `By that`, `basically`, `actually tested this`, unless rewritten as a human response;
- generic reactions: `That's fascinating`, `Exactly`, `Interesting`;
- textbook nouns unless a character immediately needs them for the demonstration in front of them.

Bad:
```txt
HOST: So the intuitive answer is actually wrong?
HOST: So the intuitive answer is actually wrong?
BUDDY: Model. Model. Look at the model.
BUDDY: Model. Model. Look at the model.
```

Good:
```txt
HOST: Okay, more traffic lanes means less traffic. Obviously.
BUDDY: Then why do wider highways get just as jammed?
HOST: ...bad luck?
BUDDY: Watch what happens when you add one more lane.
HOST: More cars showed up. That's not fair.
BUDDY: The lane didn't fix the jam. It invited more cars into it.
HOST: So building our way out of traffic just builds more traffic.
```

## Short does not mean telegraphic

The single most common way this goes wrong: compressing a line into a clipped noun-phrase pair -- something you'd jot in notes, not say out loud. It reads as a caption summarizing the idea, not a character having it.

Bad -- these are grammatically incomplete, no one talks like this:
```txt
HOST: Same tank, opposite verdicts.
BUDDY: Same water, smaller lump.
HOST: Wider road, same jam.
```

Good -- same information, same short length budget, but an actual sentence a person would say:
```txt
HOST: Same tank, but they go opposite ways.
BUDDY: Maybe freezing just packs the same water tighter.
HOST: We widened the road and got the same jam back.
```

A short line can still drop a word or two the way real speech does ("Bad luck?" "Not fair." "Obviously.") -- the test is whether it's something a person would actually blurt out, not whether it happens to be missing a subject or verb by rule.

## Useful fact style

Only one line may carry a compact idea, and it must sound spoken. Avoid formal labels unless the character immediately needs the term for what's in front of them.

Bad:
```txt
BUDDY: This is called induced demand.
```

Good:
```txt
BUDDY: More room just means more people are willing to drive.
```

Bad:
```txt
BUDDY: Researchers tested this across dozens of highway expansions.
```

Good:
```txt
BUDDY: Every time a city adds lanes, the same thing happens.
```

## Visible action and the visual model

Every scene needs an observable action in `point`. `narration` is dialogue only; `point` tells the downstream director what to show.

`point` is always a normal JSON string value, like every other field on the scene object -- `"point": "..."`, with the compact structure below as its *content*, not as bare unquoted text sitting where a JSON key belongs. A scene object never has a key that starts with `action=` or `point=`; it has a key named exactly `point`, and that key's *value* is the compact string:

```txt
action=<visible action>; prop=<central object or none>; function=<beat function>; value=<what this beat proves>
```

`function` must be one of: `hook`, `intuitive_answer`, `objection`, `visual_model`, `correction`, `implication`, `takeaway practical_action`, `recap confirms_understanding`. Every episode is checked mechanically for this: the FIRST scene tagged with each function must appear in exactly this order and never earlier. Concretely:

- `objection` and `visual_model` may each repeat (revisit them if the idea has more than one layer), but every `objection`/`visual_model` scene comes before the first `correction` scene.
- `correction` may repeat, but every `correction` scene comes after the first `visual_model` scene and before the first `implication` scene. Reserve `correction` for scenes at or after the real mechanism reveal -- an early clarifying aside is still `objection`, not `correction`, even if a character corrects a small misconception in passing.
- `implication` may repeat, but every `implication` scene comes after the first `correction` scene. Never use `implication` as a general "here's an interesting consequence" tag earlier in the episode.
- Every `takeaway practical_action` scene comes before every `recap confirms_understanding` scene -- the recap is always last, never takeaway-recap-takeaway, and never recap before takeaway.
- The single LAST scene in the whole episode is `recap confirms_understanding`, and its narration substantively restates the corrected idea (not a generic summary or "lesson learned" line).

The visual model beat is where comprehension actually happens — make it a concrete, physical demonstration using `central_object`, not a character stating a fact. If the idea needs a room change, comparison, or a before/after to actually click, show it.

Good point examples, shown as the actual JSON field they must produce:

```json
"point": "action=Host points at the crowded new lane, confused; prop=highway; function=objection; value=the intuitive answer visibly fails"
```

```json
"point": "action=Buddy lines up two toy cars then adds a third lane of cars behind them; prop=toy-cars; function=visual_model; value=viewer sees why extra room fills up instead of emptying out"
```

```json
"point": "action=Host repeats the corrected idea back while pointing at the model; prop=toy-cars; function=recap confirms_understanding; value=the viewer can now explain it in the same words"
```

Bad -- both for the wrong content and for missing the `"point": "..."` wrapper that makes this a valid JSON field instead of a stray key:

```txt
explain induced demand
action=Host thinks about traffic; prop=brain; function=summary; value=viewer learns the concept
action=Host nods; prop=none; function=middle; value=viewer sees a nod
```

## Prop and object contract

Choose one `central_object` — a physical object, model, or comparison that embodies the idea — and reuse it across the hook, the visual model, and the recap rather than introducing a new unrelated object each scene. If the topic genuinely needs a spatial demonstration (a room change, a before/after location), stage it visibly: `location A -> the change -> location B -> the corrected idea lands`. Do not force a room change when the idea doesn't need one.

## Story shape

Use the comprehension arc as the light narrative frame:

- a character states the intuitive answer, confident and reasonable;
- the other character (or a demonstration) surfaces the objection;
- the visual model makes the real mechanism concrete;
- the correction lands, built directly on the gap the objection exposed;
- one honest implication follows;
- the final exchange is the recap: a character correctly restates the idea, proving it landed.

Facts must surface because a character is actively working through the demonstration, doubts it, or is surprised by it. If a fact could be swapped for any unrelated fact without changing the interaction, rewrite it.

## Output

Produce a flat list of spoken scenes covering every story act in order. Each scene is one spoken line.

Each scene object has exactly these six fields and no others:

- `scene_index`: continuous from 0.
- `act_index`: the source act.
- `speaker`: cast character id.
- `emotion`: one of `neutral`, `happy`, `angry`, `surprised`, `sad`, `scared`. `neutral` is for genuinely flat delivery, not a default when unsure -- most lines carry some real reaction (surprise at the objection, happiness at the reveal, mild frustration at being wrong). Keep `neutral` under half the episode's lines.
- `narration`: exactly what the character says. No stage directions.
- `point`: a normal JSON string, `"point": "action=...; prop=...; function=...; value=..."` — never a bare unquoted fragment and never a key of its own.

Do not add any other field to a scene object -- no `duration`, `camera`, `mood`, `id`, `title`, `beat`, `label`, `description`, `dialogue`, `line`, `text`, `character`, `location`, or anything else, even if it feels informative. Every field beyond the six listed above fails validation and blocks the whole episode.

Hit each act's `target_words` within about 10%. Do not pad. Default cartoon explainers should feel like 90-115 seconds unless the source story explicitly requires longer.

## Final self-check before output

Before returning, count and audit:

- no exact repeated caption lines;
- no near-duplicate lines within five scenes;
- no adjacent repeated object chant like `Charger. Charger.`;
- at least half of spoken lines are 10 words or fewer, and every one of those short lines is still a real spoken sentence, not a clipped noun-phrase pair (re-read each short line and ask: would a person actually say this out loud?);
- at least one short interruption appears every 5-7 lines;
- contractions appear naturally;
- no line ends with `...`;
- no line uses `is called`, `this means`, `the reason is`, `research shows`, `studies show`, or `in other words`;
- every scene has a proper `"point": "action=...; prop=...; function=...; value=..."` field -- a real JSON key named `point` whose value is that string, never the string itself sitting where a key belongs -- with a `function` from the required list above, in roughly the required order;
- the visual model beat is a physical demonstration, not a stated fact;
- the final line is a character correctly restating the corrected idea, not a summary or a "lesson learned" line.
