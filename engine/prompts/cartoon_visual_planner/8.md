## Foreground prop staging

The latest quality target is that the viewer must see the central object, not merely hear characters talk about it. Use the script `point` metadata when available: `action=...; prop=...; function=...; value=...`.

For every scene:

- `primary_prop`: the central visible object/interface in this scene, or `none`.
- `prop_state`: the visible state of that object, or `none`.
- `prop_motion`: one of `none`, `pulse`, `glow`, `tremble`, `slide-away`, `thumb-hover`, `open`, `close`, `bounce`.
- `foreground_action`: the visible thing the object does or the character does to it, or `none`.

For phone/app/message topics, the phone/screen must appear in the opening, return in the middle, and resolve in the payoff. Good states include:

- `phone-unlocked`
- `lock-screen-glow`
- `notification-badge`
- `face-down`
- `thumb-hover`
- `across-room`
- `phone-visible`

Good phone foreground actions include:

- `Host freezes with phone already unlocked`
- `Phone lights up while Host reaches back`
- `Buddy slides the phone across the table`
- `Host reaches, notices, and grabs the kettle instead`

For other object topics, use similarly concrete states: `letter-unopened`, `bill-on-table`, `door-half-open`, `window-closeup`, `kettle-replacement`, `locker-humming`, `banana-browning`, `microwave-mesh-closeup`.

A `screen-change` visual event alone is not enough if the scene is about a phone. Pair it with `primary_prop: "phone"`, a specific `prop_state`, and a `foreground_action`.

## Direct the situation, not a talking-head podcast

The script should contain a concrete viewer-relatable situation. Put the characters in the location where that situation makes sense whenever possible.

Examples:
- late-night notification / overthinking -> bedroom at night;
- work message / presentation / boss -> office;
- test pressure -> classroom;
- social awkwardness / date -> cafe;
- waiting outside / walking -> park or street;
- household habit -> kitchen or living room;
- airplane seat, cabin, or window-seat story -> airplane-cabin;
- airport boarding/setup beat -> airport-gate;
- object-design, pressure, stress, cracking, rounded-corner, or mechanism explanation -> engineering-lab.

Avoid `generic-room` unless the script genuinely supplies no usable context. A whole episode of the same generic two-shot is a failure even if every line is technically staged.

Keep location continuity across a real back-and-forth, but allow a motivated location change when the story shifts from the triggering situation to an example, memory, consequence, payoff, or mechanism explanation. Do not teleport every line. Keep the location variant stable as well: do not flip a room from day to night merely because the emotional tone changes.

For object/design/mechanism topics, open in the real-world place where the character encounters the object, then move to the mechanism location when the explanation needs a diagram. Example: airplane-window topic should usually open in `airplane-cabin/day`, move to `engineering-lab/day` for pressure/stress/corner explanation, and return to `airplane-cabin/day` for the payoff.

An episode that never leaves its opening location is not automatically a failure, but when the script's `point` field marks an imagined scenario, a memory, a hypothetical, a mechanism, or a metaphor, stage it as a `metaphor-cutaway`, `thought-bubble`, or a motivated environment change rather than just continuing the same two-shot with new dialogue.

## Shot rhythm

Use:
- `two-shot` as the conversational base;
- `speaker-closeup` for a confession, reveal, strong claim, or punchline;
- `listener-closeup` when the reaction is more entertaining than the explanation.

Across a normal episode, use more than one framing type. A useful rhythm is two-shot -> close/reaction -> two-shot, not closeup -> closeup -> closeup.

Camera motion:
- `static` for most normal beats;
- `push-in` for a reveal or emotional realization;
- `pull-out` for deflation/awkwardness;
- `pan-left` / `pan-right` only when composition supports it.

Do not move the camera merely because the previous scene was static. Do not stack a closeup and `push-in` unless the story genuinely earns both.

## Visual events

`visual_event` adds one small deterministic event to a scene. Use it for story-motivated punctuation, not random decoration.

Allowed values:
- `none`: normal dialogue line;
- `alarm-pulse`: body alarm, panic, danger signal, sudden realization;
- `screen-change`: phone, computer, presentation screen, chart, document, diagram, or notification beat;
- `audience-silhouette`: public speaking, classroom, meeting, group judgment, being watched;
- `metaphor-cutaway`: ancient brain, predator, wolf, alarm panel, machine, pressure diagram, stress map, habit loop, or other explanatory metaphor;
- `prop-tremble`: shaking hand, paper, phone, cup, bill, letter, or small nervous object;
- `thought-bubble`: internal worry, mistaken belief, mental simulation;
- `reaction-pop`: short comedic or surprised reaction;
- `callback-card`: a callback to an earlier joke, phrase, object, or payoff.

Use at least one visual event in the opening third and one in the back half for a normal episode. A 90+ second episode should not go more than four scenes without either a visual event, a closeup, a foreground-prop state change, or a location/context change.

`cutaway_label` is the short readable label for `metaphor-cutaway`, `thought-bubble`, or `callback-card`. Use `""` for all other events unless the foreground prop needs a very short visible label. Keep labels under 8 words. Good labels: `"ANCIENT ALARM"`, `"NOT A WOLF"`, `"STRESS FINDS CORNERS"`, `"SLOT MACHINE REWARD"`.

When the script explains a mechanism with its own metaphor, give that metaphor a `metaphor-cutaway` and a label specific enough to picture as a small cartoon image. Reuse the script's own imagery where possible.

## Ambient motion

`ambient_motion` keeps backgrounds alive without creating chaos.

Allowed values:
- `none`: use only for a deliberately still beat;
- `subtle-parallax`: default for ordinary scenes;
- `window-light`: rooms with windows, airplane cabin windows, reflective pause;
- `monitor-glow`: office, computer, phone, presentation, notification, airport screens;
- `chart-wiggle`: charts, classroom boards, engineering diagrams, finance, measurement, explanation beats;
- `clock-tick`: waiting, deadlines, anxiety, time pressure;
- `rain-window`: night street, bedroom, worry, melancholy;
- `dust-float`: quiet room, awkward silence, pause before a decision.

Do not leave every scene at `none`. Static backgrounds across a whole episode are a failure. Every background should either have visible ambient motion or be paired with a story-motivated visual event/framing/foreground-prop change.

## Speaker readability

`speaker_emphasis` tells the renderer how hard to identify the active speaker.

Allowed values:
- `scale-pop`: default; active speaker gets a tiny performance lift;
- `rim-glow`: useful in busy or dark compositions;
- `listener-dim`: useful in two-shots where the listener is not the joke;
- `caption-anchor`: use when the spoken line is short and the caption should do more work;
- `none`: only for a one-character closeup.

Every normal two-character dialogue line should use one of `scale-pop`, `rim-glow`, `listener-dim`, or `caption-anchor`.

## Acting vocabulary

Emotion:
`neutral`, `happy`, `amused`, `skeptical`, `confused`, `concerned`, `sad`, `angry`, `surprised`, `scared`, `thinking`, `annoyed`.

Gesture:
`idle`, `explain`, `point-left`, `point-right`, `shrug`, `hands-open`, `surprised`, `thinking`, `facepalm`, `celebrate`.

Most lines should be `idle`. `explain` is useful but should not become the default raised-arm pose on every sentence. Bigger gestures are punctuation.

Gaze:
`auto`, `camera`, `left`, `right`, `up`, `down`, `away`.

Use `auto` normally. `camera` is for a deliberate fourth-wall beat only.

Listener reactions matter. Do not copy the speaker's emotion onto the listener. A skeptical look, delayed concern, suppressed amusement, or surprised reaction can carry the scene without extra dialogue.

## Continuity

Preserve stable cast identity, coherent emotional carry-over, location continuity during the same exchange, consistent day/night variant, stable left/right relationship, reaction logic, and the central object's opening/middle/payoff continuity.

Do not manufacture visual chaos for variety. Variety comes from story-motivated framing, reactions, location context, ambient motion, visual events, foreground prop state changes, and occasional camera emphasis.

## Confidence

Score exactly:
- `concreteness`
- `variety`
- `visual_momentum`
- `speaker_readability`

`variety` should be low if the episode is effectively the same two-character composition repeated line after line, or if every scene shares the exact same `background_location` and `background_variant` with no cutaways, foreground-prop changes, or mechanism/background changes breaking it up. `visual_momentum` should be low if the middle has no visual events, foreground-prop state changes, or motivated framing changes, or if a script-supplied triggering object never appears as a foreground prop. `speaker_readability` should be low if active-speaker emphasis is absent in two-character scenes.
