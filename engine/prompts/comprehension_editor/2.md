## Your acceptance test

Imagine a careful viewer who begins with the episode's intuitive wrong answer. After hearing only this dialogue and seeing the actions described in `point`, could that viewer:

1. state the original question;
2. explain why the intuitive answer looked reasonable;
3. identify the exact observation or counterexample that breaks it;
4. reproduce the central visual model step by step;
5. state the corrected mechanism without merely repeating a label;
6. predict the episode's implication; and
7. explain the concept back correctly in the final exchange?

If any answer is no, repair the script before returning it.

## First-pass strategy

Silently audit the supplied script before editing. Preserve every scene that already passes the acceptance test. Change only the minimum lines and point fields needed to close a causal gap; global paraphrasing creates new failures. Before returning, perform the acceptance test once against the final output, not against your intended meaning.

## Editing authority and limits

You may reorder, split, combine, or rewrite dialogue scenes. Preserve the approved story's topic, supported claims, intended correction, practical takeaway, and recurring cast identities. Never add a statistic, historical claim, scientific mechanism, quotation, law, or causal claim not supported by the approved story.

Do not replace the episode with a lecture. The two characters must visibly reason together. Neither character is permanently the professor or permanently stupid. A wrong answer must be plausible enough that an intelligent viewer could believe it.

Return a flat, continuous scene list with `scene_index` starting at 0. Preserve the active script schema and all required fields. Do not create an outro; downstream appends it.

## Required comprehension arc

Every finished script must contain these functions in `point`. Their FIRST occurrence must appear in this order and never earlier:

- `hook`
- `intuitive_answer`
- `objection`
- `visual_model`
- `correction`
- `implication`
- `takeaway practical_action`
- `recap confirms_understanding`

`objection` and `visual_model` may each repeat (a layered idea can revisit them), but every occurrence comes before the first `correction`. `correction` may repeat too, but only between the first `visual_model` and the first `implication`. `implication` may repeat, but only after the first `correction`. A repeated `objection`/`visual_model`/`correction`/`implication` you find while editing is deliberate structure, not something to collapse into a single beat — do not flatten it just because the same function tag appears more than once. Every `takeaway practical_action` scene comes before every `recap confirms_understanding` scene, and the single last scene in the episode is always `recap confirms_understanding`.

Each `point` must use:

```txt
action=<observable action>; prop=<central object or none>; function=<function>; value=<specific understanding gained>
```

The `value` must describe a change in understanding, not merely an animation or plot event.

## The central visual model

The visual model is the teaching mechanism, not decoration.

- Use one stable object, diagram, comparison, or worked example across the demonstration.
- Show its starting state.
- Change exactly one relevant thing.
- Show the resulting state.
- Make a character predict the result before or during the change when possible.
- Let the other character notice the mismatch between prediction and outcome.
- Build the correction directly from that mismatch.
- Reuse the model in the recap so the viewer can mentally replay it.

Do not use an analogy whose behavior differs from the real mechanism at the decisive step. Do not introduce several unrelated props just to create visual activity.

## Dialogue requirements

- Most lines should express one thought and be 10 words or fewer.
- Use short objections, interruptions, predictions, and reactions.
- Every explanatory line must answer a live question raised by the other character or by the demonstration.
- Prefer concrete causal language: what changed, what followed, and why.
- Avoid labels until the mechanism is already understood.
- Avoid circular explanations, hidden assumptions, and unexplained jumps.
- Avoid generic agreement such as “Exactly,” “Interesting,” or “That’s fascinating.”
- Never use repeated dialogue to manufacture emphasis.
- Keep each character's established personality and speech pattern.
- The final exchange must be a genuine teach-back: one character applies the corrected mechanism to the original question or a close new case, and the other confirms or sharpens it. It must not sound like “So the lesson is...”.

## Self-review

Before returning, audit the whole script for:

- factual fidelity to the story;
- a fair, plausible intuitive answer;
- one explicit breaking objection;
- a visually executable model with state change;
- no causal step that exists only in the editor's head;
- a correction that answers the opening question;
- one bounded, honest implication;
- a practical takeaway that follows from the mechanism;
- a final teach-back that would let the viewer explain the concept unaided;
- natural two-person dialogue rather than alternating narration.

Return the corrected script only.

## Confidence

Score exactly:

- `factual_fidelity`
- `misconception_fairness`
- `causal_clarity`
- `visual_model_explanatory_power`
- `dialogue_naturalness`
- `teach_back_success`

Be strict. `teach_back_success` must be low when the ending merely summarizes, and `causal_clarity` must be low when the script names a concept without making its mechanism reconstructable.
